## Short answers

### a) Testing the difference in AUC (or another discrimination measure) between nested models

What the literature says, in one line each:
- Pepe, Kerr, Longton and Wang 2013 prove that the null hypotheses "no improvement in performance" (for AUC, net benefit, NRI and similar measures) are all equivalent to one simple null: the new predictor Y is not a risk factor given the baseline X, P(D=1|X,Y)=P(D=1|X). Testing for improvement is therefore redundant once Y is shown to be a risk factor. Standard AUC-difference tests that ignore the variability of the estimated regression coefficients are "extremely conservative". They recommend testing Y as a risk factor (Wald or likelihood ratio test of the coefficient) and treating performance measures as estimation, with confidence intervals.
- Demler, Pencina and D'Agostino 2012 show, with simulations and a generalised U-statistic argument, that when the added predictor is not associated with the outcome the null distribution of the DeLong statistic is non-normal, so the test is conservative for small and moderate effects. They suggest testing association for the new predictor, and if significant, reporting the change in AUC with a confidence interval.
- Vickers, Cronin and Begg 2011: in simulations the size of the AUC test was below 0.006 in every configuration and its power was far below the likelihood ratio and Wald tests, which kept nominal size.
- Seshan, Gonen and Begg 2013: both the AUC-difference statistic and its estimated variance are seriously biased when predictions from nested regression models are used as inputs. Resampling can build a valid reference distribution, but Wald or likelihood ratio tests "remain the preferred approach".
- Chen et al. 2013 push back on the Vickers conclusion: the DeLong method tests a different hypothesis and is valid inside its design scope. They give an exact F test for the AUCs of nested linear discriminant functions and report that the likelihood ratio and Wald tests have inflated type I error in small samples. So "AUC tests are always inferior" is disputed for small samples under a Gaussian discriminant model; nobody disputes that naive DeLong on fitted nested logistic models is invalid.
- Newer (2015 to 2026) items found: Biostatistics 2016 and Statistical Methods in Medical Research 2021 papers on inference for nested-model delta-AUC (not read, see "Identified, not read"); Cook 2018 (association test first, likelihood ratio when nested; quartile not verified, so in the outside list); Vickers et al. 2023 on net benefit (below); Zhou et al. 2020 preprint on precision-recall (below).
- Precision-recall and rare events: I found NO paper that gives a valid test of the change in average precision (PR AUC) between nested models. What I found: Zhou, Lu, Brooke, Hudson and Yuan (arXiv 2020, outside venue filter) show delta-AUC and delta-AP are both weighted averages of changes in how event and non-event risk scores separate, with delta-AP weighting the high-risk region more; in their numerical study delta-AP tracks the scaled Brier score closely, while delta-AUC is negatively correlated with them at low event rates. Sofaer et al. 2019 (Methods in Ecology and Evolution) argue PR AUC is better than ROC AUC for rare binary events; it is a metric paper, not a test. A web search for an AUPRC-specific nested test found only ad hoc bootstrap intervals in applied papers (not citable as methods).
- Cross-validated statistics: Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani 2023 show naive confidence intervals for cross-validated error undercover because fold errors are correlated; they propose nested cross-validation for the variance. This bears on any test that treats out-of-fold metrics as independent (own note), and is related to Clark and West 2007 (econometrics), who give a bias-adjusted comparison of mean squared prediction error for nested models because the larger model adds noise by estimating parameters whose true value is zero.

Own note (not from the papers): the thesis null (surrogate fields, conditional on baseline features) targets the same hypothesis as the Pepe equivalence result, namely no information in velocity beyond the baseline. The papers above concern parametric coefficient tests; none addresses a surrogate-field null for spatial data.

### b) Conditional-independence tests that work with any machine-learning model

- Model-X knockoffs (Candes et al. 2018): need the covariates to be random with a KNOWN joint distribution (authors say they only have preliminary evidence for estimated distributions); control FDR for variable selection, pair with any importance statistic. Metropolized knockoff sampling (Bates et al. 2020) is the sampler for non-Gaussian cases (identified, abstract not read).
- Conditional randomization test (CRT) in Candes et al. 2018 and its fast form, the distilled CRT (Liu, Katsevich, Janson and Ramdas 2022): needs the law of X given Z (here: velocity given baseline features); makes no assumption on how Y depends on (X,Z); any test statistic is valid, so a held-out loss or an ML importance can be used. Distillation cuts refits of the costly learner.
- Holdout randomization test (Tansey et al. 2022): CRT with a train/holdout split so the model is fit once; p-value per feature; works with any black-box predictor; compared with knockoffs it had higher power in their simulations (abstract).
- Conditional permutation test (Berrett et al. 2020): replaces redrawing X by permuting observed X using a permutation distribution built from an approximate X|Z; needs the same approximation as CRT; type I error inflation is bounded by the approximation error and, for the worst-case statistic, is no larger than for CRT. Abstract does not discuss power.
- Power: Wang and Janson 2022 give asymptotic power formulas in a high-dimensional linear model for CRT and knockoffs with statistics based on marginal covariance, least squares coefficient or lasso; for their independent-covariate cases CRT probably dominates knockoffs (abstract-level). Katsevich and Ramdas 2022: the CRT with a likelihood-based statistic is the most powerful model-X test against a point alternative; they also give a resampling-free test with asymptotic validity when only the first two moments of X|Z are known.
- Leave-one-covariate-out (Lei et al. 2018): a model-free variable-importance idea inside a distribution-free prediction framework; the abstract page I could read gave no assumptions or limitations beyond the general distribution-free setting. Full text not read.
- Generalised covariance measure (Shah and Peters 2020): proves no conditional-independence test can have power against all alternatives (hardness result, without restriction on the null), so tests must be designed for the alternatives of interest; GCM uses regression residuals of X on Z and Y on Z, valid under weak conditions on the regression error rates; competitive in simulations.
- PCIT / predictive conditional independence( and Kire preprint 2017, arXiv, outside the filter): casts independence testing as supervised-learning model comparison; abstract states no formal assumptions. Watson and Wright 2021 (Machine Learning): conditional predictive impact, uses knockoffs with any learner and loss; simulations show type I control and nominal coverage; relies on valid knockoff or conditional sampling.
- Common assumption, therefore: either know (CRT, knockoffs, CPT) or estimate well (GCM, doubly robust variants) the distribution of the tested variable given the covariates. The Li and Liu 2022 Maxway CRT (arXiv, outside the filter) states that the model-X CRT may lose validity if X|Z is misspecified. (Own note: a variogram-matched surrogate field is one way to draw X|Z; none of these papers treat spatially dependent, non-iid rows. Katsevich and Ramdas's and Berrett's results are for iid sampling.)

### c) Power and sample size with rare outcomes and case-control / nested case-control sampling

Little was found that answers the question directly.
- No paper found that compares the power of conditional logistic regression with unconditional logistic regression with sampled controls for TESTING ADDED PREDICTIVE VALUE.
- Secondary-source statements (web-search summaries, not read at source): for conditional logistic regression on sampled risk sets, efficiency relative to the full cohort is about 1/2 with one control per case and about m/(m+1) with m controls under the null; about 4 to 5 controls per case is common practice; Bertke et al. (Epidemiologic Methods, quartile not verified) simulate that more controls help most when cases are few and effects strong. I could not verify these against the original papers (Goldstein and Langholz 1992 was not located).
- Weighting for NCC risk prediction: a 2021 arXiv preprint (Zhou and colleagues) proposes inverse-probability weights when only a subset of events are selected as cases; an IPW AUC estimator for matched case-control data with parent-cohort data was reported in a 2018 paper (not identified further). Both are outside what I could read.
- Wang and Janson 2022 analyse the CRT when samples are collected retrospectively (abstract-level).
- Proper scoring rules versus rank measures as statistics: Pepe et al. 2015 (Statistics in Biosciences, Q3 by Scimago 2024, so outside the filter) state ROC-based measures, net benefit and the Brier score cannot be inflated by miscalibrated models the way NRI can. Harrell's blog (outside) argues the likelihood ratio test has far more power than AUC tests and treats log likelihood and Brier as the two proper scores. Austin and Steyerberg 2013 show the change in c-statistic, Brier score, R-squared and discrimination slope shrink as the baseline model gets stronger. Zhou 2020 (preprint): delta-AP agrees closely with delta scaled Brier at low event rates, delta-AUC does not. Gneiting and Raftery 2007 is the standard reference on strictly proper scoring rules (identified, not read).

### d) Net reclassification, decision curves and other measures as test statistics

- Kerr et al. 2014: recommend reporting NRI components (event and non-event) separately, advise against NRI with three or more categories, say regression coefficient tests are superior to NRI for testing the null of no increment, bootstrap over published variance formulas for intervals, and prefer improvement in net benefit as the single summary (abstract; the regression-test point comes from the abstract wording and a web summary).
- Hilden and Gerds 2013 (abstract via secondary source): NRI and IDI are not safe for judging added value because they are not proper scoring rules and can be inflated by poorly fitting models. Pepe, Fan, Feng, Gerds and Hilden 2015: with independent test data NRI can be positive when the new marker has no information; outside the filter (Q3).
- Pencina et al. 2012: the AUC increase depends on baseline model strength; IDI to a lesser degree; continuous NRI only on effect size and correlation; recommend reporting all three. Note this is a descriptive paper, not about test validity.
- Vickers, Van Calster, Wynants and Steyerberg 2023 (Diagnostic and Prognostic Research; quartile not verified): argue against routine confidence intervals and p-values for net benefit, and suggest value-of-information analysis instead (abstract via secondary summary).
- Decision curves and NRI: nothing found that establishes a valid, powerful hypothesis test for either.
